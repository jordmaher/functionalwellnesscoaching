Resetting

The 15-minute walk that changed how I work, rest, and reset.

Jordan Maher · June 2026 · 6 min read

I've been taking walks in nature for years. Multiple times a day, sometimes. During tedious projects when my brain starts to fog. During stressful seasons when everything feels like too much. During the kind of stress that doesn't have an obvious source or an obvious fix. During burnout, when even the simplest tasks feel impossible. I didn't have a scientific framework for it. I just knew that something changed every time I stepped outside and let myself be in green space for a few minutes.

At some point I got curious enough to look it up. What I found was too good not to share.

I didn't expect the research to be this specific

There's a psychological theory called Attention Restoration Theory, developed by researchers Rachel and Stephen Kaplan. The idea is this: screens, open offices, and busy streets demand what they call directed attention. The focused, effortful kind you use when you're solving problems, managing tasks, and making decisions. That resource runs down over the day. And when it runs out, you don't just feel tired. You become more impulsive, more irritable, and less able to focus or think ahead.

Natural settings work differently. They hold your attention gently, which gives the effortful kind a chance to recover. Researchers call this soft fascination: the easy attention you give to moving leaves or birdsong. It asks almost nothing of you. And that's exactly the point.

The stress numbers are hard to ignore

Researchers at the University of Michigan asked people to spend time in nature several times a week for eight weeks and measured cortisol, the body's main stress hormone, through saliva samples. About 20 minutes was enough for a meaningful drop, with the biggest benefit between 20 and 30 minutes. They measured a 21.3 percent per hour drop in cortisol beyond its normal daily decline.

A small 2024 study compared walking a green, tree-lined path with walking along an urban road. Cortisol fell on both walks, but by 53 percent on average on the green path, compared with 37 percent on the city road. The setting matters, not just the movement.

What this may mean for women

A 2022 study looked at activity in the amygdala, the part of the brain involved in processing stress, before and after a one-hour walk in either a forest or a busy city area. Activity went down after the walk in nature, and the drop showed up in women but not in men. The researchers suggested women may benefit more from time in nature.

One study isn't the final word. But for women carrying burnout or a long season of stress, that's worth knowing. The walk isn't only a nice-to-have. It may be one of the simplest tools you have.

"The walk isn't the break from the work. It's what makes the work possible."

How I actually use this

I don't save my walks for after I finish work. I use them as part of how I work.

When I hit a wall mid-task, I go outside. When I've been on calls for two hours, I go outside. When I feel the particular kind of tired that sleep doesn't fix, I go outside. Even ten minutes changes something. The mental static clears. I come back to my desk with a different quality of attention than I left with.

I've also found that the boring tasks, the ones that feel hardest to start, go better when I break them up with a few minutes in green space. The research points the same way: the benefit tends to be bigger for people who are already worn out.

That means the moments you most want to push through are often the exact moments you most need to step away.

The practical takeaway

You don't need a forest or a hiking trail. Much of this research took place in parks, green paths near workplaces, and city green spaces. What matters is that you're outside, near something natural, without your phone pulling your attention somewhere else.

Start with 15 minutes. Step away from the screen. Leave the podcast at home. Let your mind do the easy thing for a few minutes.

You were built for this. Give yourself a few minutes of it.
