Patterns

The $10 coffee when you're in debt.

Jordan Maher · June 2026 · 7 min read

There was a period in my life when I was in credit card debt, barely covering student loans, and spending money the moment it hit my account. Bills first, then whatever was left disappeared faster than I could track it. I was making okay money, but not enough for the lifestyle of a young woman living in downtown Chicago. I knew exactly what I was doing. And I kept doing it anyway.

The thing I remember most is the coffee. Ten dollars, every single morning. A ritual I held onto with both hands while everything else felt like it was slipping. I knew it wasn't helping. I did it anyway. And then I felt ashamed about it. And then somehow I spent more.

If you've ever been in a version of that loop, the spending when you're broke, the eating when you swore you wouldn't, the scrolling when you had real things to do, this is for you. Not because I'm going to tell you to stop. But because I want to explain what was actually happening. Because once I understood it, everything changed.

"You don't have a discipline problem. You've been trying to change without the right conditions."

Why logical advice never works

The first thing everyone says when they find out you're in debt and still spending is some version of just stop. Make a budget. Be more disciplined. And the maddening thing is that you already know all of that. You've made the budget. You've set the intentions. You've had the conversations with yourself at 2am where you swear this week will be different.

And then it isn't. And you wonder what's wrong with you.

Here's what I want you to hear clearly: nothing is wrong with you. Your brain is doing exactly what brains do under long stretches of stress. The problem isn't your character. It's the conditions you're living inside.

What's actually happening

The scarcity trap

Researchers Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir spent years studying what scarcity does to the human mind. What they found wasn't what most people expect. When we're in a state of scarcity, financial, emotional, or otherwise, our mental bandwidth narrows. The mind becomes so consumed by the immediate problem that it has less room for long-term thinking. In their research, the effect was similar in size to losing a night of sleep.

When you're in debt, your mind isn't working with its full capacity. It's running hot, focused on the immediate problem, unable to zoom out. The $10 coffee isn't irrational in that state. It's your mind reaching for the one small thing it can control. The one good moment in an otherwise overwhelming day.

The relief loop

When you're under constant stress from money, shame, or worry, your brain looks for small bursts of relief that quiet the alarm for a few minutes. Spending, eating, scrolling. Each one brings a quick lift that makes the stress feel manageable, briefly.

Your brain learns that the behavior brings relief, so it keeps returning to it, even when the logical part of you knows it makes things worse. It's the same reward loop behind most habits that are hard to break, which is why so many of them look alike from the outside.

The shame spiral

This is the piece nobody talks about. After you spend the money you told yourself you wouldn't, shame arrives. And shame isn't just a passing feeling. It can set off the same stress response your body has to a threat.

To escape that feeling, you look for relief. Which often leads to another impulsive choice. Which creates more shame. Which creates more seeking. The loop doesn't deepen because you're weak. It deepens because shame is genuinely hard to sit with, and you're trying to get through it.

The loop in plain language

  • Long stretches of stress narrow your bandwidth, so long-term thinking gets harder
  • You reach for quick relief: spending, eating, scrolling
  • The relief works for a moment, so your brain learns to repeat it
  • Shame arrives after, and it's hard to sit with
  • You look for relief from the shame, often through the same behavior
  • The hole gets deeper, not because of weakness, but because of how habits work

The what-the-hell effect

Psychologists studying dieting discovered something they eventually called the what-the-hell effect. Once someone breaks a rule, eats something off plan, spends money they shouldn't have, misses a workout, the mind shifts into a specific mode. It says I've already failed. I might as well go all the way.

This is why one bad financial day can turn into a month of spending. Why eating one cookie can turn into eating the whole bag. It's not about the cookie or the purchase. It's about what the mind does the moment it registers failure. Most people take this as proof they have no self-control. What it actually is is a predictable pattern that happens to almost everyone under the right conditions.

Knowing it has a name changes something. You're not uniquely broken. You're human.

Why the hole gets deeper when there seems to be no way out

There's one more piece I want to name because it explained my experience the most precisely. It's called temporal discounting: the tendency to value immediate rewards far more than future ones. Most of the time, people can balance present pleasure against future consequences reasonably well. Under constant stress, that balance tips toward the present.

When the future feels hopeless, the mind discounts it almost entirely. If there is no realistic path to being out of debt, why would your brain factor debt into its decisions? It won't. It will focus on now. On the coffee. On the thing that makes this moment feel okay. The deeper the hole feels, the more your mind puts the present first. This isn't a flaw. It's survival logic working in the wrong context.

"The coffee was never about the coffee. It was the only good thing happening in the next ten minutes."

What actually helps

I want to be honest here. I'm not going to give you a budgeting tip. Not because budgets are useless. They're not. But they're step three in a sequence that most people try to start at step one.

The sequence that works for me, and for the women I've guided, is this: feeling safe enough first, noticing the pattern second, changing the behavior third.

It's very hard to build lasting change while you're running on stress and shame. So the first work is creating enough calm that you can think clearly again. Sleep. Water. Movement that feels good rather than punishing. Less shame. These aren't soft extras. They're what makes change possible.

Noticing the pattern comes second. Not judging it, getting curious about it. When does it happen? What's the feeling right before? What does the loop give you, even for a moment? Understanding a pattern with compassion isn't the same as excusing it. It's what makes it possible to interrupt it.

Then, and only then, does changing the behavior have a real chance of sticking.

Three things worth trying this week

  • Name the loop without judgment the next time it happens. "I'm looking for relief" is more useful than "I have no willpower."
  • Ask what the behavior is giving you, not what it's costing you. The answer shows you what you actually need.
  • Say the shame out loud. Talk to someone you trust, or write it down. The loop loses power once it's named.

Why I'm writing about this

I spent years thinking the coffee was a character flaw. That the loop I was in said something unfixable about me. It took understanding what was happening underneath to realize it said something very fixable about my conditions.

I've guided women who are doing the same thing I did. Spending when they're broke. Eating when they swore they wouldn't. Reaching for their phone the moment discomfort arrives. And the first thing I want every one of them to understand is that the loop isn't who they are. It's what happens when someone has been running on stress for a long time.

That's what we work on. Not discipline. Not willpower. Not another habit tracker. We work on creating the conditions where change actually becomes possible.

The $10 coffee makes complete sense when you understand what it was really for. And once you understand that, really understand it and not just in your head, you don't need the coffee the same way anymore.
